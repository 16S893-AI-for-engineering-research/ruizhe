01

CONTEXT

Background

Worldwide, fossil-fuel power plants emit roughly one fifth of all anthropogenic nitrogen oxides (NOx) [1]. In the United States, continuous emissions monitoring systems (CEMS) measure them at the stack every hour. Most plants elsewhere carry no such instruments.

Inventories must instead infer emissions from fuel statistics and assumed emission factors. Satellites can check those inventories from above: TROPOMI on Sentinel-5P maps nitrogen dioxide (NO2), the visible form of NOx, at a resolution of a few kilometers—fine enough to see the plume drifting downwind of a large plant.

Earlier this year, we built a pipeline that finds plumes in TROPOMI scenes, assigns each plume to its source plant, and uses a convolutional neural network to estimate the plant's hourly NOx emission rate [2]. The network reads the NO2 field together with wind, cloud, viewing geometry, and surface information. It was trained and tested on U.S. plants with hourly CEMS records as ground truth; every test plant was held out from training.

THE PROJECT ASKS

  1. How many overpasses make a plant-level estimate reliable?
  2. Which plants can the pipeline quantify well, and why?
  3. Can sparse overpasses support annual emission totals?
  4. Can the method transfer beyond the United States?
02

ERROR DECOMPOSITION

Reliability, limits, and overpass count

A satellite estimate behaves like a bathroom scale: repeated measurements average away random jitter, but they cannot remove a systematic offset.

Let the random error be σε and the systematic error be σb. For an average over N overpasses, the mean squared error is:

MSE(N)=σb2 + σε2N01

The two components call for different remedies: random error asks for more overpasses; systematic error asks for better calibration. Current test results give a root-mean-square error of 957 lb h−1 per overpass and 544 lb h−1 per plant, with roughly 100 overpasses per plant. Equation 1 implies σε ≈ 790 and σb ≈ 540 lb h−1.

The main suspect is the regression slope of about 0.45: the model under-predicts large emitters and over-predicts small ones. We will estimate both error components directly from held-out residuals, separately for small, medium, and large emitters, and test Equation 1 by averaging random subsets of overpasses.

CALIBRATE

Correct the slope

Test whether regression dilution [3] drives shrinkage toward the average plant, then correct plant means using the fitted slope.

QUANTIFY

Conformal intervals

Calibrate distribution-free uncertainty [4, 5] at the plant—not overpass—level to preserve shared plant bias.

DELIVER

Overpass rule

Report how many observations a plant needs to reach a target precision, such as ±30%.

Physical floor. A plume becomes visible only when its NO2 enhancement exceeds retrieval noise. At a wind speed of 5 m s−1, one pixel corresponds to about 600 lb h−1; the threshold rises in proportion to wind speed. We will test whether errors increase toward this floor.

03

OPERATING ENVELOPE

Which plants can be quantified well—and why?

Seven test plants are not enough to explain performance. Five-fold cross-validation at the plant level will expand the well-observed evaluation cohort from 7 to 46 plants.

Each fold holds out one fifth of the plants, and all training—including pretraining—excludes them. Every plant therefore receives a prediction from a model that has never seen it. We will model error as a function of three groups of candidate drivers:

01

Plant

Capacity, fuel, controls, operating consistency, and distance from cities or nearby sources.

02

Scene

Wind, cloud, sun angle, surface brightness, aerosol, and viewing conditions.

03

Sampling

Overpass count, timing, and temporal clustering.

Results will be reported by stratum rather than only overall because drivers are entangled in the U.S. fleet: large coal plants cluster in the dry, bright interior West, where retrievals are cleanest. The main product will be an operating envelope—a map of emission magnitude against overpass count showing where calibrated error remains below a chosen threshold.

Channel ablations and counterfactual wind rotations will also test what the network has learned: does it use wind as the physics requires, or has it found a shortcut?

04

FROM HOURS TO YEARS

Can the pipeline deliver annual emissions?

Inventories and regulators think in years. A year-by-year evaluation must separate model error from uneven satellite sampling.

TROPOMI passes at about 13:30 local time, only clear skies yield a retrieval, and only a running plant produces a plume. The mean over observed hours can therefore differ from the true annual mean. We will decompose the error exactly:

P − A=(P − S)+(S − A)02
P Model mean over sampled hoursS CEMS mean over the same hoursA True annual CEMS mean

The first term is model error. The second is sampling representativeness; CEMS alone determines it for every plant and year, and it applies to any satellite method. It should remain small for steadily running plants and grow for intermittent plants.

Four annual estimators will be scored against CEMS totals for 2019–2024:

  1. Plain meanAverage all sampled overpasses.
  2. Non-detection awareCount scenes with no detected plume as evidence of low emissions.
  3. Portable correctionUse globally available signals such as plume-detection frequency.
  4. Pooled estimateBorrow strength across years when one year has few observations.

Year-over-year change receives a separate test. Subtracting two years for the same plant can cancel plant-specific systematic error, potentially making a retrofit, retirement, or the 2020 slowdown easier to detect than the absolute level.

05

TRANSFER TEST

Toward the global fleet: a 2018 pilot

The pilot will test the pipeline where hourly CEMS does not exist—without claiming a full global catalog.

2018 CONSTRAINTS

8 monthsRoutine TROPOMI operations began in May.
7 kmPixels were longer and coarser than the training scenes.
1 versionData must use a reprocessed retrieval consistent with training [6].

The United States in 2018 tests all three effects against CEMS. If skill declines, we will retrain with images blurred to the coarser pixel size. The global step will then apply the model to selected large plants outside the United States, drawn from public plant databases [7].

Each plant must pass two checks. First, is it within the model's experience? Plants with unfamiliar emission magnitudes or scene conditions will be labeled in-support, marginal, or out-of-support; quantitative results will be reported only for the first two. Second, does the estimate agree with annual facility totals available in the European Union, Australia, Canada, and South Africa?

INDEPENDENT CHECK

A physics-based mass-balance estimate will be computed on the same scenes alongside the neural-network result.
